## 28.09 - (Cyber)bullying prevention
- Bullying involves repeated harm and a power imbalance. Group dynamics and bystanders matter.
- Cyberbullying extends beyond school. Harmful content can persist and spread.
- Take the targeted person seriously. Listen, preserve evidence and involve trusted adults.
- Do not place responsibility on the victim. Adults must coordinate protection and intervention.
- Involve students in prevention, while keeping adults responsible for handling situations.
- Act early and follow up. Address the group climate, not only individual behaviour.
- Do not leave the targeted person alone.

## 06.10 - Tech. responsibility
- Designers are responsible for the tools and powers they give players.
- Technology is not neutral: rules, roles and rewards shape behaviour.
- Appeal and responsibility can conflict: attracting children and satisfying investors may create different priorities.

**Potential bullying within this system.**
- Appearance and identity: mocking clothing, sharing humiliating images or impersonating another player.
- Group hierarchies: exclusion, scapegoating and harassment linked to gang membership, roles or status.
- Economic pressure: scams, coercive trades and discriminatory pricing.
- Abuse of role-specific powers: manipulating prices, repeatedly imprisoning someone or deliberately abandoning passengers.
- Persistent obstruction: following, blocking, shoving or repeatedly preventing someone from participating.
- Status manipulation: damaging or stealing clothing, targeting reputation and potentially exploiting the “Slay-Score.”

**Narrative and interaction**
- Chat connects the fictional world with real social relationships. Its effects can extend beyond play.
- Diegetic elements belong within the fictional world: ex: a message received on a character’s in-world phone.

## 12.10 - Storytelling importance
**Design not only what happens, but how people experience it and give it meaning.**

- Build a world, characters and a plot. Establish the setting, its rules and the possibilities for action.
- Show, don’t tell. Reveal characters through their actions, desires and decisions rather than explanations.
- Make motivations understandable. What matters is knowing what a character wants and why it matters to them.
- Use contradictions and obstacles. Internal struggles and external challenges shape characters and their development.
- Stories do not always need a central conflict. Kishōtenketsu offers another structure: introduction, development, turn and conclusion.
- Interaction can carry the narrative. In playable stories, mechanics, choices and exploration influence how players experience events.
- Consider perspective and responsibility. Storytelling can encourage empathy, but also reinforce stereotypes or manipulate audiences.

## 13.10 - STORYTELLING ADVICES
**Developing a Story**

- Start with your strongest idea. Do not save it for the ending.
- Let the ending emerge. It may differ from what you originally imagined.
- Stay flexible. Avoid forcing the story towards a predetermined conclusion.
- Reframe the problem. If you cannot find a satisfying solution, reconsider the problem itself.
- Let a strong beginning open possibilities rather than making every step serve a fixed ending.
